These short, original retellings introduce a few well-known episodes from the Mahabharata, Ramayana, and Vishnu Purana. They are summaries in fresh wording, not translations or replacements for the source texts.

Many traditions, many tellings. Hindu epics and sacred narratives are living traditions, shared in different languages, regions, communities, and artistic forms. These brief versions follow selected older textual sources; details and interpretations can differ. The Archive.org item you shared is linked for reading, but its edition is not reproduced here.

Mahabharata

Ekalavya's lesson

Adi Parva · a story of skill, exclusion, and difficult choices

Ekalavya, a young archer from the Nishada community, hoped to learn from the royal teacher Drona. Turned away, he made an image of the teacher and practiced alone until his aim became extraordinary. When Drona later discovered his skill, he asked Ekalavya for his right thumb as a teacher's gift. Ekalavya gave it, even though the demand changed what he could do with a bow. The episode is remembered not only for discipline, but also for the unequal access to learning and the painful cost of loyalty.

Consider: Who gets access to learning, and who pays when a system protects privilege?

Draupadi's choice

Adi Parva · a contest at her svayamvara

At a gathering where princes sought Draupadi's hand, the challenge demanded unusual focus: a contestant had to use a powerful bow to strike a target set above. Many who tried could not complete it. Arjuna, present in a Brahmin's disguise, succeeded. The events that followed brought Draupadi into the Pandava family's shared life and set the stage for later questions about choice, kinship, and duty. Retellings differ in how they present Draupadi's agency and the contest's meaning.

Consider: How can a person's own choice be respected amid family expectations?

The question in the dice hall

Sabha Parva · a turning point in the epic

A gambling match draws Yudhishthira into a chain of losses, until Draupadi herself is called into the assembly. Rather than accept the court's demand, she asks whether a person who has already lost his own freedom can wager another person. The powerful elders struggle to answer, and their silence becomes part of the wrong. The scene exposes the danger of treating people as property and the responsibility of those who witness injustice. It helps deepen the conflict that later leads to war.

Consider: What responsibility do bystanders have when someone is treated unjustly?

Arjuna pauses before battle

Bhishma Parva · the setting of the Bhagavad Gita

As the armies gather, Arjuna sees relatives, teachers, and friends on both sides. The thought of fighting them overwhelms his confidence, and he lowers his weapon. Krishna, serving as his charioteer, answers with a long teaching about action, responsibility, disciplined understanding, and devotion. Listeners and traditions interpret the dialogue in many ways. Its dramatic starting point remains a human one: a warrior stops before a life-changing act and asks how to proceed when every path carries a cost.

Consider: How can someone think clearly when duty and compassion seem to pull in different directions?

Savitri and the path of Yama

Vana Parva · a tale of resolve and wisdom

Savitri chooses Satyavan as her husband, knowing from a sage's warning that his life will be short. When Yama comes to lead Satyavan's spirit away, Savitri follows, answering the god with patience and thoughtful words. Yama offers her boons, and she asks for the well-being of her family and the restoration of what has been lost. Through her careful requests, she ultimately secures Satyavan's return. The tale celebrates devotion and intelligence while inviting readers to notice how Savitri speaks and acts for herself.

Consider: When facing a hard truth, what can steady resolve and clear speech make possible?

Ramayana

A promise and a forest exile

Ayodhya and Aranya traditions · the beginning of Rama's years away

A royal promise changes the future of Ayodhya: Rama is sent away from the kingdom for years, and Bharata is named to rule in his place. Rama accepts the exile; Sita and Lakshmana choose to accompany him. In the forest, the three leave courtly life behind and meet both hospitality and danger. The episode is told in many forms, and readers have long discussed its ideas of promises, power, loyalty, and the freedom of each person to choose a path.

Consider: How should a promise be weighed when keeping it deeply affects other people?

Hanuman finds Sita

Sundara Kanda · a journey to Lanka

When the search for Sita reaches the ocean, Hanuman takes on the daring crossing to Lanka. He searches the city carefully and finally finds her in a grove, guarded and far from home. Instead of asking her to trust a stranger, he offers a sign connected to Rama and carries her message back. His strength matters, but so do patience, judgment, and the care he takes to reassure her. The journey turns a desperate search into a clear path for the next part of the story.

Consider: How can courage be paired with patience and respect for another person's safety?

Selected Puranic stories

Dhruva's steady devotion

Vishnu Purana · a child seeks lasting purpose

After feeling rejected within his family, the young Dhruva turns away from courtly rivalry and seeks a purpose that will not depend on another person's approval. He enters the forest and devotes himself to focused prayer. Vishnu appears and grants him an honored place among the stars. Different tellings emphasize different lessons: perseverance, devotion, a child's hurt, or the transformation of ambition into spiritual purpose.

Consider: What lasting purpose could help you move beyond the need to prove yourself?

The ocean is churned

Vishnu Purana · devas and asuras work toward amrita

In a cosmic struggle, devas and asuras agree to churn the ocean in search of amrita, the nectar of immortality. Mount Mandara becomes the churning rod and the serpent Vasuki the rope. The work brings forth many wonders—and danger—before the nectar appears. The story is not simply a tale of easy victory: cooperation is uneasy, the rewards are contested, and the powerful must decide what to do with what emerges. Its details vary across Puranic tellings.

Consider: What unintended consequences should people prepare for when they pursue a valuable goal together?

Krishna and Govardhana

Vishnu Purana · a village shelter in a storm

When a fierce storm threatens the people and cattle of Vraja, Krishna raises Govardhana Hill and shelters the community beneath it. The scene is remembered as a display of divine protection, and as a challenge to pride and fear. The people survive not as isolated individuals but as a community gathered together. Interpretations of the episode differ among traditions and tellings.

Consider: What does it mean to protect a community when the danger is bigger than one person?
